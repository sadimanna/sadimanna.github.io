<p>Standard subword tokenizers — such as Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE), WordPiece, and SentencePiece — are trained predominantly on English-heavy web corpora. As a result, non-English and low-resource languages suffer from severe <strong>token fragmentation</strong> and heavy <strong>tokenization premiums</strong>. Sentences in non-Latin or morphologically rich low-resource languages often require 3× to 5× (and in extreme cases like Shan, up to 19×) more tokens to encode than their English equivalents. This structural inequality inflates computational latency, drastically shrinks effective context windows, and increases monetary API and energy costs.</p>
<p>To address these challenges, recent research has explored diverse strategies — ranging from language-specific tokenizer benchmarking and data augmentation to inference-time token merging, dynamic programming, hypernetwork retrofitting, and test-time training. Below, we break down these approaches, examining how each operates and analyzing their specific shortcomings.</p>
<h3 id="introduction"><a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden tabindex="-1" href="#introduction"><span class="anchor-icon">#</span></a>Introduction</h3>
<p>At the heart of the low-resource tokenization crisis is a fundamental design flaw: <strong>static, globally optimized vocabularies</strong>. Because standard tokenizers assign subwords based on global frequency across massive pretraining sets, high-resource languages (especially English) monopolize the available vocabulary slots. When presented with text from a low-resource language, the tokenizer lacks longer subwords or root morphemes, forcing it to fall back on inefficient byte-level or character-level splits.</p>
<p>Beyond operational costs, over-segmentation degrades semantic modelling. Arbitrary subword breaks split compound words, verb roots, and case markers, forcing neural networks to spend capacity reassembling fragmented tokens rather than performing downstream reasoning.</p>
<p>Researchers have proposed several paradigms to tackle this problem:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Language-Specific Static Tokenizers</strong>: Training subword algorithms from scratch on target low-resource corpora.</li>
<li><strong>Data Augmentation</strong>: Generating synthetic training pairs to boost compositional generalization in low-data regimes.</li>
<li><strong>Test-Time &#x26; Dynamic Tokenization</strong>: Adapting token boundaries, segmentations, or embeddings on the fly during inference without retraining the underlying foundation model.</li>
<li><strong>Vocabulary Retrofitting &#x26; Sensitivity Mitigation</strong>: Adding missing character tokens or post-training models to handle non-canonical tokenization robustly.</li>
</ol>
<p>While these approaches offer promising avenues for cross-lingual fairness, each carries distinct technical trade-offs and structural limitations.</p>
<h3 id="discussion"><a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden tabindex="-1" href="#discussion"><span class="anchor-icon">#</span></a>Discussion</h3>
<p>Below is a systematic breakdown of eleven key works in low-resource and test-time tokenization, outlining their core mechanisms and critical shortcomings.</p>
<h3 id="1-comparative-analysis-of-subword-tokenizers-fordzongkha"><a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden tabindex="-1" href="#1-comparative-analysis-of-subword-tokenizers-fordzongkha"><span class="anchor-icon">#</span></a>1. Comparative Analysis of Subword Tokenizers for Dzongkha</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Core Contribution</strong>: Wangchuk &#x26; Gonsalves (2025) present an empirical evaluation of Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE), WordPiece, and SentencePiece (Unigram) trained from scratch on a 27-million character corpus of Dzongkha — the low-resource national language of Bhutan. Their study demonstrates that <strong>SentencePiece (Unigram)</strong> is the most effective tokenizer for Dzongkha, achieving the lowest Normalized Sequence Length (NSL), the lowest subword fertility (0.79 vs. 13.37 for GPT-2), and the fastest encoding execution time (131 ms per loop).</li>
<li><strong>Shortcomings &#x26; Limitations</strong>:</li>
<li><strong>High Initial Training Time</strong>: SentencePiece required significantly longer time during the initial vocabulary construction/pretraining phase (over 2 minutes for 10k/30k vocabularies) compared to WordPiece and BPE (2–46 seconds).</li>
<li><strong>Lack of Extrinsic Evaluation</strong>: The study evaluated tokenizers purely on intrinsic metrics (sequence length, subword fertility, execution time) on a single isolated dataset, without measuring performance on downstream NLP tasks such as machine translation, sentiment analysis, or document classification.</li>
<li><strong>Incompatibility with Pretrained LLMs</strong>: Training a language-specific tokenizer from scratch requires pretraining a new LLM from scratch, which does not solve the problem of adapting existing, large-scale foundation models.</li>
</ul>
<h3 id="2-geca-good-enough-compositional-data-augmentation"><a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden tabindex="-1" href="#2-geca-good-enough-compositional-data-augmentation"><span class="anchor-icon">#</span></a>2. GECA: Good-Enough Compositional Data Augmentation</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Core Contribution</strong>: Andreas (2020) introduces GECA, a model-agnostic, rule-based data augmentation protocol. GECA identifies substitutable sentence fragments occurring in identical surrounding contexts across a training set and synthesizes new training examples by swapping those fragments. Applied to low-resource language modeling across six typologically diverse languages (e.g., Kinyarwanda, Pashto, Lao, Tok Pisin), GECA consistently reduces n-gram language model perplexity.</li>
<li><strong>Shortcomings &#x26; Limitations</strong>:</li>
<li><strong>Linguistically Imprecise</strong>: GECA relies on a crude substitution heuristic that frequently violates linguistic rules, generating semantically unacceptable or ungrammatical sentences in roughly 49% of synthesized cases.</li>
<li><strong>Domain &#x26; Task Dependence</strong>: Gains are restricted to datasets with high structural and compositional recursion (e.g., SCAN or GeoQuery). On datasets with lower recursion or nested query structures (such as SCHOLAR text-to-SQL), GECA yields 0% improvement.</li>
<li><strong>Shallow Smoothing Strategy</strong>: GECA acts as an n-gram smoothing mechanism rather than addressing the root cause of subword over-segmentation or vocabulary bias in neural tokenizers.</li>
</ul>
<h3 id="3-optimal-bpe-segmentation-via-dynamic-programming"><a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden tabindex="-1" href="#3-optimal-bpe-segmentation-via-dynamic-programming"><span class="anchor-icon">#</span></a>3. Optimal BPE Segmentation via Dynamic Programming</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Core Contribution</strong>: Raj et al. (2025) challenge the standard greedy segmentation used in BPE tokenizers. They propose an <strong>Optimal BPE Segmentation algorithm</strong> that formulates tokenization as a dynamic programming problem (similar to the Viterbi algorithm) over a Trie built on reversed vocabulary tokens. This finds the globally minimal token count for a document given a fixed vocabulary, achieving a 3–5% overall Token Saving Ratio (TSR) and up to 20% token savings on rare/complex words while preserving morpheme and compound boundaries in languages like Turkish, Hindi, and Tamil.</li>
<li><strong>Shortcomings &#x26; Limitations</strong>:</li>
<li><strong>Bound by Fixed Vocabulary</strong>: The algorithm optimizes segmentation <em>only</em> over existing vocabulary entries. If a target language’s core morphemes or subwords were never included in the original vocabulary, dynamic programming cannot synthesize them.</li>
<li><strong>Algorithmic Complexity</strong>: Although the worst-case time complexity is (O(NM)) (matching greedy BPE in theory), constructing the Trie and performing DP lattice backtracking adds operational overhead compared to standard greedy merging.</li>
<li><strong>Narrow Extrinsic Validation</strong>: Extrinsic task evaluations were conducted primarily on four languages (English, Finnish, Indonesian, Turkish) using small model scales (120M and 350M parameters), leaving foundation-scale LLM behavior unproven.</li>
</ul>
<h3 id="4-retrofitting-large-language-models-with-dynamic-tokenization"><a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden tabindex="-1" href="#4-retrofitting-large-language-models-with-dynamic-tokenization"><span class="anchor-icon">#</span></a>4. Retrofitting Large Language Models with Dynamic Tokenization</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Core Contribution</strong>: Feher et al. (2025) present a framework to retrofit subword-based LLMs with dynamic tokenization. Inspired by BPE, their method merges frequent adjacent subwords within input batches on the fly at test time and uses a pretrained <strong>hypernetwork (HN)</strong> to predict vector embeddings for the newly formed tokens on demand. For encoder models (XLM-R), this reduces token sequence lengths by >20% across 14 languages with &#x3C;2% loss in accuracy; for decoders (Mistral-7B), it reduces prefilling sequence length by up to 17%.</li>
<li><strong>Shortcomings &#x26; Limitations</strong>:</li>
<li><strong>Computational Overhead</strong>: Generating batch-specific dynamic vocabularies and running hypernetwork passes for runtime embeddings incurs computational overhead.</li>
<li><strong>Hypernetwork Dependency &#x26; Training Cost</strong>: Relying on an auxiliary hypernetwork requires access to a pretrained hypernetwork; training a new hypernetwork for a 7B model requires substantial compute (~3 days on a TPUv4–32 pod).</li>
<li><strong>Intractable Autoregressive Generation</strong>: True dynamic tokenization is straightforward for prefilling and scoring (where full text is available upfront), but applying it directly to open-ended autoregressive decoding is computationally intractable. Restricting output generation to a static, bounded 1M-token vocabulary causes degradation (e.g., a 5.9% drop on MMLU).</li>
</ul>
<h3 id="5-zip2zip-inference-time-adaptive-tokenization-via-online-compression"><a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden tabindex="-1" href="#5-zip2zip-inference-time-adaptive-tokenization-via-online-compression"><span class="anchor-icon">#</span></a>5. zip2zip: Inference-Time Adaptive Tokenization via Online Compression</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Core Contribution</strong>: Geng et al. (2025) introduce zip2zip, a framework that brings context-adaptive tokenization to LLMs at inference time using the <strong>Lempel–Ziv–Welch (LZW)</strong> lossless compression algorithm. During decoding, zip2zip dynamically identifies recurring token sequences in the context window, merges them into compact <strong>hypertokens</strong>, and uses a lightweight hyper-encoder (e.g., a 1- or 2-layer transformer) to compute their embeddings on the fly. This achieves 15–40% token sequence reductions and up to 40% decoding speedups across multilingual, code, and math domains.</li>
<li><strong>Shortcomings &#x26; Limitations</strong>:</li>
<li><strong>Low Hypertoken Reuse &#x26; Memory Overhead</strong>: LZW codebooks grow linearly with context length, but empirical analysis reveals that ~75% of created hypertokens are never reused during generation, resulting in inefficient memory usage.</li>
<li><strong>Degradation on Numerical Reasoning</strong>: While general NLP benchmark accuracy remains stable, zip2zip exhibits noticeable degradation on numerical reasoning tasks (e.g., GSM8K), likely because dynamic token merging interferes with digit-level token operations.</li>
<li><strong>Optimization Trade-Offs</strong>: Pretraining on compressed sequences introduces optimization challenges, leading to a slight increase in byte-level perplexity. Furthermore, hypertoken generation requires strict synchronization between input embedding and output unembedding layers to avoid decoding errors.</li>
</ul>
<h3 id="6-post-hoc-character-retrofitting-for-non-latin-scripts"><a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden tabindex="-1" href="#6-post-hoc-character-retrofitting-for-non-latin-scripts"><span class="anchor-icon">#</span></a>6. Post-Hoc Character Retrofitting for Non-Latin Scripts</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Core Contribution</strong>: Churchill &#x26; Skiena (2026) analyze tokenization premiums across ten popular LLMs, demonstrating that non-Latin script languages (e.g., Bangla, Hindi, Urdu) face 3× to 5× penalties relative to English. To mitigate this without costly retraining, they propose retrofitting frozen models by post-hoc addition of missing single-character Unicode tokens, deriving their embeddings by averaging or projecting multi-token layer representations from the base model.</li>
<li><strong>Shortcomings &#x26; Limitations</strong>:</li>
<li><strong>Output “Vote-Splitting” Problem</strong>: Adding new character tokens alongside original sub-character tokens splits output probability mass between old and new encodings during generation, potentially causing incorrect token selection unless explicitly constrained.</li>
<li><strong>Surrogate Evaluation Metric</strong>: Performance was evaluated using last-hidden-state cosine similarity between compressed and uncompressed inputs rather than direct downstream task accuracy.</li>
<li><strong>Limited Scope</strong>: The method addresses only individual missing Unicode characters (character-level retrofitting); it does not solve multi-character morpheme over-segmentation or multi-word fragmentation.</li>
</ul>
<h3 id="7-langmap-language-adaptive-map-tokenization"><a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden tabindex="-1" href="#7-langmap-language-adaptive-map-tokenization"><span class="anchor-icon">#</span></a>7. LangMAP: Language-Adaptive MAP Tokenization</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Core Contribution</strong>: Meister et al. (2026) introduce <strong>Language-adaptive Maximum a Posteriori (LangMAP) Tokenization</strong>, an approach extending the UnigramLM framework. LangMAP maintains a collection of language-specific unigram probability distributions over a <strong>single, fixed shared vocabulary</strong>. At inference time, it evaluates maximum-likelihood segmentations across all language distributions and selects the optimal path without requiring explicit language tags. Across 11 tokenizers and 9 natural languages, LangMAP significantly improves morphological boundary alignment (MorphScore) and AST-leaf alignment in code.</li>
<li><strong>Shortcomings &#x26; Limitations</strong>:</li>
<li><strong>Constrained by Base Vocabulary Coverage</strong>: LangMAP re-weights existing vocabulary items but cannot create new tokens. If a script’s characters or subwords are missing from the base vocabulary (e.g., Devanagari in English-centric LLMs), probability re-weighting alone cannot fix over-segmentation.</li>
<li><strong>Requires Labeled Data for Estimation</strong>: Fitting per-language distributions requires labeled monolingual corpora during the offline EM estimation phase.</li>
<li><strong>Mixed Downstream Impact</strong>: Fine-tuning gains were observed primarily on grammatical acceptability (MultiBLiMP), whereas knowledge-heavy benchmarks (Belebele and Global-PIQA) showed no significant improvement.</li>
</ul>
<h3 id="8-sensitivity-to-non-canonical-tokenization-in-low-resource-languages"><a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden tabindex="-1" href="#8-sensitivity-to-non-canonical-tokenization-in-low-resource-languages"><span class="anchor-icon">#</span></a>8. Sensitivity to Non-Canonical Tokenization in Low-Resource Languages</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Core Contribution</strong>: Zheng et al. (2026) conduct a systematic study revealing that LLM robustness to non-canonical (alternative) tokenizations — previously observed in English — <strong>fails to generalize to low-resource languages</strong>. Higher token fragmentation directly correlates with severe performance drops under non-canonical segmentations (e.g., up to 40% degradation for Llama-3.1–8B). The authors show that LoRA fine-tuning using multi-tokenization data augmentation (sampling across granularity buckets) significantly improves cross-lingual tokenization robustness.</li>
<li><strong>Shortcomings &#x26; Limitations</strong>:</li>
<li><strong>Diagnostic &#x26; Training Focus</strong>: The paper provides a diagnostic framework and fine-tuning data-augmentation technique, rather than proposing an active inference-time tokenizer architecture to eliminate over-segmentation in the first place.</li>
<li><strong>Incomplete Mitigation</strong>: Even after multi-tokenization training, heavily fragmented character-level segmentations still cause performance losses compared to canonical tokenization.</li>
<li><strong>Limited Task Evaluation</strong>: Multi-tokenization training as a data-augmentation strategy was evaluated primarily on Multilingual-ARC.</li>
</ul>
<h3 id="9-search-over-tokens-soto-1d-ordered-token-structures"><a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden tabindex="-1" href="#9-search-over-tokens-soto-1d-ordered-token-structures"><span class="anchor-icon">#</span></a>9. Search-over-Tokens (SoTo): 1D Ordered Token Structures</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Core Contribution</strong>: Gao et al. / Bachmann et al. (2026) investigate test-time scaling in autoregressive generative models through <strong>1D coarse-to-fine ordered token structures</strong> (such as FlexTok). They prove that coarse-to-fine token ordering creates a searchable latent space where early token prefixes capture global semantic concepts, enabling effective beam search and verifier-guided steering.</li>
<li><strong>Shortcomings &#x26; Limitations</strong>:</li>
<li><strong>Domain Restriction</strong>: Designed specifically for visual/image tokenization and text-to-image autoregressive generation, rather than natural low-resource human language processing.</li>
<li><strong>Detokenization Latency Bottleneck</strong>: Verifier-guided search requires repeatedly decoding intermediate token prefixes into images using flow-based detokenizers, creating a wall-clock latency bottleneck during beam search.</li>
<li><strong>Verifier &#x26; Quality Dependencies</strong>: Search effectiveness depends heavily on the accuracy of external verifiers (e.g., CLIP, ImageReward) and early token prefix reconstruction quality.</li>
</ul>
<h3 id="10-test-time-training-with-next-token-prediction-ttt-ntp"><a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden tabindex="-1" href="#10-test-time-training-with-next-token-prediction-ttt-ntp"><span class="anchor-icon">#</span></a>10. Test-Time Training with Next-Token Prediction (TTT-NTP)</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Core Contribution</strong>: Ouyang et al. (2026) present <strong>Test-Time Training with Next-Token Prediction (TTT-NTP)</strong>, an in-place adaptation framework for long-context LLMs. Rather than relying on separate value proxies, TTT-NTP updates MLP down-projection fast weights at test time using a layer-local target: the same layer’s next-position contextual hidden state produced during the causal forward pass. Across Llama-3.1–8B and Mistral-7B, TTT-NTP improves RULER long-context retrieval scores by up to 3.9 points and LongBench-v2 QA by up to 5.6 points.</li>
<li><strong>Shortcomings &#x26; Limitations</strong>:</li>
<li><strong>Context Retrieval Focus</strong>: Designed to solve long-context retrieval degradation within nominal context windows, rather than addressing vocabulary representation or subword over-segmentation in low-resource scripts.</li>
<li><strong>Requires Continual Pretraining</strong>: Adapting the fast weights requires an initial continual pretraining (CPT) phase to learn the fast-weight write projection matrix ((\mathbf{W}_{\text{proj}})) before inference deployment.</li>
<li><strong>Architecture Constraints</strong>: The closed-form fast-weight solver relies on global key statistics across prompt chunks, making extension to sliding-window or linear-attention backbones non-trivial.</li>
</ul>
<h3 id="11-learning-a-continue-thinking-token-for-test-time-scaling"><a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden tabindex="-1" href="#11-learning-a-continue-thinking-token-for-test-time-scaling"><span class="anchor-icon">#</span></a>11. Learning a Continue-Thinking Token for Test-Time Scaling</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Core Contribution</strong>: Ringel et al. (2025) explore test-time compute scaling for reasoning models (e.g., DeepSeek-R1 distillations). Instead of replacing end-of-thinking tags (</think>) with fixed words like "Wait", they train a dedicated &#x3C;|continue-thinking|> token embedding using reinforcement learning while keeping model weights frozen. Forced insertion of this token extends chain-of-thought reasoning traces, yielding up to a 4.2% absolute accuracy improvement on math benchmarks like GSM8K and MATH500.</li>
<li><strong>Shortcomings &#x26; Limitations</strong>:</li>
<li><strong>Reasoning-Specific Scope</strong>: Focuses strictly on chain-of-thought reasoning budget forcing for math and logic, offering no mechanism for general multilingual text tokenization or vocabulary adaptation.</li>
<li><strong>Training &#x26; API Restrictions</strong>: Requires RL training to optimize the token embedding (requiring model weight access) and cannot be deployed through commercial APIs that prohibit vocabulary modifications.</li>
<li><strong>Contingent Baseline Effectiveness</strong>: The learned token provides gains only on tasks where standard fixed-token budget forcing already works; on extremely difficult benchmarks where budget forcing fails (e.g., AIME), the learned token yields no statistically significant benefit.</li>
</ul>
