<p><img src="/images/blog/the-practical-guide-to-distributed-training-using-pytorch-pa/img1.jpeg" alt=""></p>
<p>Welcome back to our series on distributed training with PyTorch! In previous installments, we explored the fundamentals, culminating in a deep dive into <em><code>DistributedDataParallel</code></em> (DDP) in <a href="https://medium.com/the-owl/the-practical-guide-to-distributed-training-using-pytorch-part-3-on-multiple-nodes-using-c07b6dcf4001">Part 3</a> and <a href="https://medium.com/the-owl/the-practical-guide-to-distributed-training-using-pytorch-part-4-on-multiple-nodes-using-slurm-83cf306a3373">Part 4</a> of our series. DDP is a powerhouse, but it has one fundamental limitation: your entire model, its gradients, and its optimizer states must fit into the memory (VRAM) of a single GPU.</p>
<p>In the era of Large Language Models (LLMs) and massive vision transformers with hundreds of billions of parameters, this limitation is no longer theoretical — it’s a hard wall. So, what do you do when your model is simply too big for any single device?</p>
<p>You distribute <em>everything</em>. Welcome to <strong>Fully Sharded Data Parallelism</strong> (FSDP).</p>
<h3 id="what-is-fsdp-and-why-do-we-needit"><a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden tabindex="-1" href="#what-is-fsdp-and-why-do-we-needit"><span class="anchor-icon">#</span></a>What is FSDP, and Why Do We Need It?</h3>
<p>FSDP is a paradigm shift from DDP. Instead of replicating the entire model on each GPU, FSDP shards (or partitions) the model’s parameters, gradients, and optimizer states across all the GPUs in your distributed group.</p>
<p>Let’s visualize the difference:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>DDP</strong>: Imagine four workers building a car. Each worker has a complete, identical copy of the massive car blueprint. They work on their assigned car part and then average their findings. The problem? If the blueprint is too big to fit in one worker’s station, the whole operation fails.</li>
<li><strong>FSDP</strong>: Now, imagine the same four workers. Instead of a full blueprint, each worker gets only one-quarter of it. Worker 1 gets the engine schematics, Worker 2 the chassis, and so on. When a worker needs to assemble a part that requires information from another’s blueprint section, they quickly communicate to get just the necessary piece, use it, and then discard it.</li>
</ul>
<p>This “<strong>just-in-time</strong>” fetching of model parameters is the magic of FSDP. It dramatically reduces the peak memory footprint on each GPU, allowing you to train models that are orders of magnitude larger than what would fit on a <strong>single device</strong>.</p>
<h3 id="how-fsdp-works-the-intricate-dance-of-communication"><a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden tabindex="-1" href="#how-fsdp-works-the-intricate-dance-of-communication"><span class="anchor-icon">#</span></a>How FSDP Works: The Intricate Dance of Communication</h3>
<p>FSDP’s memory savings come from a clever sequence of communication operations (<em><code>all\_gather</code></em>, <em><code>reduce\_scatter</code></em>) that are precisely timed with computation.</p>
<p>Here’s a simplified step-by-step breakdown of a forward and backward pass for a single FSDP-wrapped layer:</p>
<ol>
<li>
<p><strong>Initial State</strong>: Each GPU (let’s say we have 4) holds only its 1/4 shard of the model’s parameters. The memory usage is low.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p><strong>Forward Pass</strong>:</p>
</li>
</ol>
<ul>
<li><strong>All-Gather</strong>: Just before the <em><code>forward()</code></em> method of a specific layer is called, FSDP triggers an <em><code>all\_gather</code></em> operation. All GPUs share their parameter shards for <em>that specific layer</em>, so each GPU momentarily reconstructs the full parameters for just that layer.</li>
<li><strong>Computation</strong>: The forward pass for that layer is executed on the full parameters.</li>
<li><strong>Discard</strong>: Immediately after the forward pass is complete, the gathered, non-local parameter shards are discarded. The GPU’s memory is freed, returning to its low state. This process repeats layer by layer.</li>
</ul>
<ol start="3">
<li><strong>Backward Pass</strong>:</li>
</ol>
<ul>
<li>The process happens in reverse. To compute the gradients for a layer, we first need its full parameters.</li>
<li><strong>All-Gather</strong>: Again, an <em><code>all\_gather</code></em> operation reconstructs the full parameters for the current layer.</li>
<li><strong>Gradient Computation</strong>: The backward pass is executed, calculating the gradients for the full layer.</li>
<li><strong>Reduce-Scatter</strong>: This is the key step. Instead of each GPU holding the full gradients, a <em><code>reduce\_scatter</code></em> operation is performed. It averages the gradients across all GPUs and shards them simultaneously. Each GPU ends up with only its corresponding shard of the final, averaged gradient.</li>
<li><strong>Discard</strong>: The full parameters and non-local gradient shards are discarded, freeing memory.</li>
</ul>
<p>This dance ensures that the full parameters for any given layer only exist in VRAM for the brief moment they are needed for computation. The optimizer states are also sharded, so each GPU only needs to store and update the states corresponding to its parameter shard.</p>
<h3 id="the-practical-guide-implementing-fsdp"><a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden tabindex="-1" href="#the-practical-guide-implementing-fsdp"><span class="anchor-icon">#</span></a>The Practical Guide: Implementing FSDP</h3>
<p>Let’s get our hands dirty. The good news is that if you’re familiar with DDP, the transition to FSDP is surprisingly manageable.</p>
<h4 id="step-1-standard-distributed-setup"><a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden tabindex="-1" href="#step-1-standard-distributed-setup"><span class="anchor-icon">#</span></a>Step 1: Standard Distributed Setup</h4>
<p>This part is identical to DDP. You need to initialize the process group.</p>
<pre><code class="hljs language-python"><span class="hljs-keyword">import</span> os 
<span class="hljs-keyword">import</span> torch 
<span class="hljs-keyword">import</span> torch.distributed <span class="hljs-keyword">as</span> dist 
<span class="hljs-keyword">from</span> torch.distributed.fsdp <span class="hljs-keyword">import</span> FullyShardedDataParallel <span class="hljs-keyword">as</span> FSDP 
<span class="hljs-keyword">from</span> torch.distributed.fsdp.wrap <span class="hljs-keyword">import</span> size_based_auto_wrap_policy 
<span class="hljs-keyword">import</span> functools  

<span class="hljs-keyword">def</span> <span class="hljs-title function_">setup</span>(): 
    <span class="hljs-comment"># These variables are set by torchrun </span>
    local_rank = <span class="hljs-built_in">int</span>(os.environ[<span class="hljs-string">"LOCAL_RANK"</span>]) 
    rank = <span class="hljs-built_in">int</span>(os.environ[<span class="hljs-string">"RANK"</span>]) 
    world_size = <span class="hljs-built_in">int</span>(os.environ[<span class="hljs-string">"WORLD_SIZE"</span>]) 
    dist.init_process_group(<span class="hljs-string">"nccl"</span>, rank=rank, world_size=world_size) 
    torch.cuda.set_device(local_rank)  

<span class="hljs-comment"># Call this at the beginning of your script </span>
setup()
</code></pre>
<h4 id="step-2-the-fsdp-wrapper-and-auto-wrapping"><a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden tabindex="-1" href="#step-2-the-fsdp-wrapper-and-auto-wrapping"><span class="anchor-icon">#</span></a>Step 2: The FSDP Wrapper and Auto-Wrapping</h4>
<p>This is where things change. Instead of wrapping your model with <em><code>DDP</code></em>, you wrap it with <em><code>FSDP</code></em>. However, for FSDP to work optimally, it needs to know <em>how</em> to group layers into sharding units. This is done via an <em><code>auto\_wrap\_policy</code></em>.</p>
<p>The most common policy is <em><code>size\_based\_auto\_wrap\_policy</code></em>, which groups layers together until they exceed a certain size threshold. For Transformer-based models, there is also a <em><code>transformer\_auto\_wrap\_policy</code></em>.</p>
<pre><code class="hljs language-python"><span class="hljs-keyword">from</span> my_models <span class="hljs-keyword">import</span> MyLargeTransformer 
<span class="hljs-comment"># Your model definition  </span>
<span class="hljs-comment"># Define the auto-wrap policy </span>
<span class="hljs-comment"># This tells FSDP to create a new FSDP unit for any layer (and its children) </span>
<span class="hljs-comment"># that has more than 100M parameters. Adjust this value based on your model. </span>
auto_wrap_policy = functools.partial(size_based_auto_wrap_policy, 
                                    min_num_params=<span class="hljs-number">100_000_000</span>  )  

<span class="hljs-comment"># Instantiate your model </span>
<span class="hljs-comment"># CRITICAL: For very large models, instantiate on the 'meta' device first </span>
<span class="hljs-comment"># to avoid OOM on rank 0 before sharding. </span>
<span class="hljs-keyword">with</span> torch.device(<span class="hljs-string">"meta"</span>): 
    model = MyLargeTransformer(vocab_size=<span class="hljs-number">50000</span>, d_model=<span class="hljs-number">4096</span>, nhead=<span class="hljs-number">32</span>, num_layers=<span class="hljs-number">24</span>)  

<span class="hljs-comment"># Wrap the model with FSDP </span>
<span class="hljs-comment"># The model is now sharded across all available GPUs. </span>
fsdp_model = FSDP(model, auto_wrap_policy=auto_wrap_policy)
</code></pre>
<h4 id="step-3-the-optimizer-the-criticalorder"><a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden tabindex="-1" href="#step-3-the-optimizer-the-criticalorder"><span class="anchor-icon">#</span></a>Step 3: The Optimizer (The Critical Order)</h4>
<p>This is the most common point of failure for beginners. You MUST create the optimizer <em>after</em> wrapping the model with FSDP.</p>
<p>FSDP replaces the model’s parameters with special <em><code>FlatParameter</code></em> objects. The optimizer needs to be initialized with these sharded parameters, not the original full ones.</p>
<pre><code class="hljs language-text"># WRONG - This will fail! 
# optimizer = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=1e-4) 
# fsdp_model = FSDP(model, ...)  

# CORRECT 
fsdp_model = FSDP(model, ...) 
# Wrap first 
optimizer = torch.optim.Adam(fsdp_model.parameters(), lr=1e-4) 
# Then create optimizer
</code></pre>
<h4 id="step-4-saving-and-loading-checkpoints"><a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden tabindex="-1" href="#step-4-saving-and-loading-checkpoints"><span class="anchor-icon">#</span></a>Step 4: Saving and Loading Checkpoints</h4>
<p>You cannot use a simple <em><code>torch.save(model.state\_dict())</code></em> on rank 0. Why? Because rank 0 only has its <em>shard</em> of the model, not the whole thing! FSDP provides specific contexts for handling state dicts.</p>
<p>There are two main strategies:</p>
<ol>
<li>Full State Dict: Gathers the entire model onto a single rank (usually rank 0) and saves it as a single file. This is simple but can cause OOM on the CPU if the model is enormous.</li>
<li>Sharded State Dict: Each rank saves its shard as a separate file. This is the most memory-efficient and scalable method.</li>
</ol>
<pre><code class="hljs language-python"><span class="hljs-keyword">from</span> torch.distributed.fsdp <span class="hljs-keyword">import</span> StateDictType, FullStateDictConfig, ShardedStateDictConfig  
<span class="hljs-comment"># --- SAVING --- </span>
<span class="hljs-comment"># Option 1: Full State Dict (saved only on rank 0) </span>
<span class="hljs-keyword">if</span> dist.get_rank() == <span class="hljs-number">0</span>: 
    <span class="hljs-built_in">print</span>(<span class="hljs-string">"Saving full model checkpoint..."</span>) 

<span class="hljs-comment"># Define the policy for gathering the full state dict </span>
full_state_dict_config = FullStateDictConfig(offload_to_cpu=<span class="hljs-literal">True</span>, rank0_only=<span class="hljs-literal">True</span>) 
<span class="hljs-keyword">with</span> FSDP.state_dict_type(fsdp_model, StateDictType.FULL_STATE_DICT, full_state_dict_config): 
    cpu_state = fsdp_model.state_dict() 
    <span class="hljs-keyword">if</span> dist.get_rank() == <span class="hljs-number">0</span>: 
        torch.save(cpu_state, <span class="hljs-string">"full_model_checkpoint.pt"</span>)  

<span class="hljs-comment"># Option 2: Sharded State Dict (each rank saves its part) </span>
<span class="hljs-built_in">print</span>(<span class="hljs-string">f"Saving sharded model checkpoint on rank <span class="hljs-subst">{dist.get_rank()}</span>..."</span>) 

<span class="hljs-comment"># Define the policy for sharded state dict </span>
sharded_state_dict_config = ShardedStateDictConfig(offload_to_cpu=<span class="hljs-literal">True</span>) 
<span class="hljs-keyword">with</span> FSDP.state_dict_type(fsdp_model, StateDictType.SHARDED_STATE_DICT, sharded_state_dict_config): 
    sharded_state = fsdp_model.state_dict() 
    <span class="hljs-comment"># Each rank saves its own shard </span>
    torch.save(sharded_state, <span class="hljs-string">f"sharded_checkpoint_rank-<span class="hljs-subst">{dist.get_rank()}</span>.pt"</span>)   

<span class="hljs-comment"># --- LOADING --- </span>
<span class="hljs-comment"># Loading follows a similar pattern. You must wrap the model in FSDP before loading the state dict.  </span>

<span class="hljs-comment"># Option 1: Loading from a Full State Dict </span>
<span class="hljs-comment"># Define the same FSDP model structure </span>
<span class="hljs-keyword">with</span> torch.device(<span class="hljs-string">"meta"</span>): 
    model_to_load = MyLargeTransformer(...) 

fsdp_model_load = FSDP(model_to_load, ...)  

<span class="hljs-comment"># Load the full state dict on rank 0 and then scatter it </span>
full_state_dict_config = FullStateDictConfig(offload_to_cpu=<span class="hljs-literal">True</span>, rank0_only=<span class="hljs-literal">True</span>) 
<span class="hljs-keyword">with</span> FSDP.state_dict_type(fsdp_model_load, StateDictType.FULL_STATE_DICT, full_state_dict_config): 
    <span class="hljs-keyword">if</span> dist.get_rank() == <span class="hljs-number">0</span>: 
        state_dict = torch.load(<span class="hljs-string">"full_model_checkpoint.pt"</span>) 
        fsdp_model_load.load_state_dict(state_dict) 
    <span class="hljs-comment"># All ranks wait for rank 0 to finish loading and scattering </span>
    dist.barrier()   

<span class="hljs-comment"># Option 2: Loading from a Sharded State Dict </span>
<span class="hljs-comment"># This is generally more robust and memory-efficient </span>
sharded_state_dict_config = ShardedStateDictConfig(offload_to_cpu=<span class="hljs-literal">True</span>) 
<span class="hljs-keyword">with</span> FSDP.state_dict_type(fsdp_model_load, StateDictType.SHARDED_STATE_DICT, sharded_state_dict_config): 
    state_dict = { 
        <span class="hljs-string">"model"</span>: fsdp_model_load.state_dict(), 
        <span class="hljs-comment"># You can also save and load optimizer state here </span>
        } 
    <span class="hljs-comment"># Each rank loads its own shard </span>
    dist_checkpoint.load_state_dict( 
        state_dict, 
        storage_reader=dist_checkpoint.FileSystemReader(<span class="hljs-string">f"sharded_checkpoint_rank-<span class="hljs-subst">{dist.get_rank()}</span>.pt"</span>), 
    ) 
    fsdp_model_load.load_state_dict(state_dict[<span class="hljs-string">"model"</span>])
</code></pre>
<p><em>Note: The sharded checkpointing API has evolved. The modern approach uses <code>torch.distributed.checkpoint</code> which is more robust. For the latest best practices, always consult the official PyTorch documentation:</em> <a href="https://pytorch.org/docs/stable/fsdp.html.*"><em>https://pytorch.org/docs/stable/fsdp.html.</em></a></p>
<h3 id="common-errors-and-their-solutions"><a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden tabindex="-1" href="#common-errors-and-their-solutions"><span class="anchor-icon">#</span></a>Common Errors and Their Solutions</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Error: <em><code>OutOfMemoryError: CUDA out of memory</code></em> during model instantiation.</strong></li>
</ol>
<ul>
<li>Cause: You are trying to create the full, multi-billion parameter model on a single GPU (rank 0) before FSDP has a chance to shard it.</li>
<li>Solution: Use the <em><code>torch.device(“meta”)</code></em> context manager. This creates a “skeleton” of the model without allocating any storage. FSDP can then take this skeleton and materialize the sharded parameters directly on each target GPU, bypassing the initial OOM.</li>
</ul>
<ol start="2">
<li><strong>Error: Optimizer errors or <em><code>ValueError: optimzier got different parameter groups</code></em>.</strong></li>
</ol>
<ul>
<li>Cause: You created the optimizer before wrapping the model with <em><code>FSDP</code></em>.</li>
<li>Solution: Always follow the correct order: 1. <em><code>model = MyModel(…)</code></em>, 2. <em><code>fsdp\_model = FSDP(model, …)</code></em>, 3. <em><code>optimizer = Adam(fsdp\_model.parameters(), …)</code></em>.</li>
</ul>
<ol start="3">
<li><strong>Error: Training is extremely slow.</strong></li>
</ol>
<ul>
<li>Cause: This can be complex, but a common reason is a suboptimal <em><code>auto\_wrap\_policy</code></em> or no policy at all. If you wrap the entire model as one giant FSDP unit, you lose the fine-grained communication/computation overlap.</li>
<li>Solution: Use an <em><code>auto\_wrap\_policy</code></em>. For Transformers, <em><code>transformer\_auto\_wrap\_policy</code></em> is highly recommended. For other architectures, experiment with <em><code>size\_based\_auto\_wrap\_policy</code></em> and adjust the <em><code>min\_num\_params</code></em> to match your model’s structure. Also, ensure your GPUs have a fast interconnect (e.g., NVLink).</li>
</ul>
<ol start="4">
<li><strong>Error: Incorrect state dict is saved or loaded.</strong></li>
</ol>
<ul>
<li>Cause: Using a naive <em><code>torch.save(model.state\_dict())</code></em>.</li>
<li>Solution: You must use the <em><code>FSDP.state\_dict\_type</code></em> context manager. For very large models, prefer the <em><code>SHARDED\_STATE\_DICT</code></em> strategy to avoid memory bottlenecks on a single rank.</li>
</ul>
<ol start="5">
<li><strong>Feature: How to save even more memory?</strong></li>
</ol>
<ul>
<li>Solution: FSDP supports <em><code>cpu\_offload</code></em>. By passing <em><code>cpu\_offload=CPUOffload(offload\_params=True)</code></em> to the FSDP wrapper, it will offload sharded parameters, gradients, and optimizer states to CPU RAM when they are not being used in the forward/backward pass. This comes at the cost of increased data transfer over the PCIe bus, so it will slow down training, but it allows for training even larger models.</li>
</ul>
<h3 id="conclusion"><a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden tabindex="-1" href="#conclusion"><span class="anchor-icon">#</span></a>Conclusion</h3>
<p>Fully Sharded Data Parallelism is the key that unlocks training for truly massive models in PyTorch. By moving beyond the replication strategy of DDP and embracing a sharding-first approach, FSDP allows you to pool the VRAM of all your GPUs into a single, massive virtual memory space.</p>
<p>While it introduces new complexities, particularly around model instantiation and checkpointing, the core concepts are a logical extension of the distributed principles we’ve already learned. By understanding the FSDP “dance” of gathering and scattering data, using the correct initialization and saving patterns, and carefully configuring wrapping policies, you can break through the memory wall and train the next generation of state-of-the-art models.</p>
