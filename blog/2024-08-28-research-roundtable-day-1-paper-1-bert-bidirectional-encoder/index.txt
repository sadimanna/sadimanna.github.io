<p>In this article, we will review the paper which introduced <strong>BERT</strong> in <em><strong>Language Models</strong></em>. This article is part of a new series of paper review articles, where we will identify the most salient points and contributions of a paper and summarise them in the form of an article.</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Please feel free to add any other points that are not mentioned below in the comments.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>The major points of the paper mentioned above can be summarized as follows:</p>
<h2 id="motivation"><a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden tabindex="-1" href="#motivation"><span class="anchor-icon">#</span></a>Motivation</h2>
<ul>
<li>The major limitation is the contemporary standard language models were unidirectional, and this limited the choice of architectures that can be used during pre-training.</li>
<li>For example, in OpenAI GPT, the authors use a left-to-right architecture, where every token can only attend to previous tokens in the self-attention layers of the Transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017).</li>
<li>Such restrictions are sub-optimal for sentence-level tasks and could be very harmful when applying finetuning-based approaches to token-level tasks such as question answering, where it is crucial to incorporate context from both directions.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="what-does-bert-do"><a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden tabindex="-1" href="#what-does-bert-do"><span class="anchor-icon">#</span></a>What Does BERT Do?</h2>
<ul>
<li>BERT alleviates the previously mentioned unidirectionality constraint by using a “<em>masked language model</em>” (MLM) pre-training objective, inspired by the Cloze task (Taylor, 1953).</li>
<li>The “<em>masked language model</em>” randomly masks some of the tokens from the input, and the objective is to predict the original vocabulary ID of the masked word based only on its context.</li>
<li>Unlike left-to-right language model pre-training, the MLM objective enables the representation to fuse the left and the right context, which allows pre-training of a deep bidirectional Transformer.</li>
<li>In addition to the masked language model, a “<em>next sentence prediction</em>” task was also used that jointly pretrains text-pair representations.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="architecture"><a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden tabindex="-1" href="#architecture"><span class="anchor-icon">#</span></a>Architecture</h2>
<ul>
<li>BERT’s model architecture is a multi-layer bidirectional Transformer encoder based on the original implementation described in Vaswani et al. (2017) and released in the tensor2tensor library.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="data-processing"><a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden tabindex="-1" href="#data-processing"><span class="anchor-icon">#</span></a>Data Processing</h2>
<ul>
<li>To make BERT handle a variety of down-stream tasks, our input representation can unambiguously represent both a single sentence and a pair of sentences (e.g., Question, Answer) in one token sequence</li>
<li>Throughout this work, a “sentence” can be an arbitrary span of contiguous text, rather than an actual linguistic sentence.</li>
<li>A “sequence” refers to the input token sequence to BERT, which may be a single sentence or two sentences packed together.</li>
<li>WordPiece embeddings (Wu et al., 2016) with a 30,000-token vocabulary are used.</li>
<li>For the pre-training corpus, the authors use BooksCorpus (800M words) (Zhu et al., 2015) and English Wikipedia (2,500M words).</li>
<li>For Wikipedia, only the text passages are extracted and the rest is ignored. It is critical to use a document-level corpus rather than a shuffled sentence-level corpus such as the Billion Word Benchmark (Chelba et al., 2013) to extract long contiguous sequences.</li>
</ul>
<h3 id="sep-token"><a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden tabindex="-1" href="#sep-token"><span class="anchor-icon">#</span></a>[SEP] Token</h3>
<ul>
<li>Sentence pairs are packed together into a single sequence. We differentiate the sentences in two ways. First, we separate them with a special token ([SEP]). Second, we add a learned embedding to every token indicating whether it belongs to sentence A or sentence B.</li>
</ul>
<h3 id="cls-token"><a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden tabindex="-1" href="#cls-token"><span class="anchor-icon">#</span></a>[CLS] Token</h3>
<ul>
<li>The first token of every sequence is always a special classification token ([CLS]). The final hidden state corresponding to this token is used as the aggregate sequence representation for classification tasks.</li>
</ul>
<h3 id="token-representation"><a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden tabindex="-1" href="#token-representation"><span class="anchor-icon">#</span></a>Token Representation</h3>
<ul>
<li>For a given token, its input representation is constructed by summing the corresponding token, segment, and position embeddings.</li>
</ul>
<p><img src="/images/blog/research-roundtable-day-1-paper-1-bert-bidirectional-encoder/img2.png" alt=""></p>
<h2 id="pre-training-objectives"><a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden tabindex="-1" href="#pre-training-objectives"><span class="anchor-icon">#</span></a>Pre-training Objectives</h2>
<ul>
<li>Unlike Peters et al. (2018a) and Radford et al. (2018), traditional left-to-right or right-to-left language models are not used to pre-train BERT. Instead, we pre-train BERT using two unsupervised tasks, mentioned below.</li>
</ul>
<h3 id="task-1-masked-lm-mlm"><a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden tabindex="-1" href="#task-1-masked-lm-mlm"><span class="anchor-icon">#</span></a>Task 1: Masked LM (MLM)</h3>
<ul>
<li>To train a deep bidirectional representation, some percentage of the input tokens are simply masked at random, and then those masked tokens are predicted. This procedure is described as a “masked LM” (MLM), although it is often referred to as a Cloze task in the literature (Taylor, 1953).</li>
<li>In this case, the final hidden vectors corresponding to the mask tokens are fed into an output softmax over the vocabulary, as in a standard LM.</li>
</ul>
<h3 id="task-2-next-sentence-prediction-nsp"><a class="heading-anchor" aria-hidden tabindex="-1" href="#task-2-next-sentence-prediction-nsp"><span class="anchor-icon">#</span></a>Task 2: Next Sentence Prediction (NSP)</h3>
<ul>
<li>To train a model that understands sentence relationships, the authors pre-train for a binarized next-sentence prediction task that can be trivially generated from any monolingual corpus.</li>
<li>Specifically, when choosing the sentences A and B for each pretraining example, 50% of the time B is the actual next sentence that follows A (labelled as <strong>IsNext</strong>), and 50% of the time it is a random sentence from the corpus (labelled as <strong>NotNext</strong>).</li>
<li>The [CLS] token is used for next-sentence prediction (NSP).</li>
<li>To fine-tune on <strong>GLUE</strong>, the first input token C ([CLS]) is used as the aggregate representation and the only new parameters introduced during fine-tuning are classification layer weights W, with K as the number of labels. The standard classification loss is calculated with C and W, i.e., log(softmax(CW^T )).</li>
</ul>
