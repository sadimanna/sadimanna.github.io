Neural Networks — Part 1: McCulloch-Pitts Neuron


The modern neural network owes its origin to the rise in computational cognitive science in the mid-20th century. The advent of computers allowed contemporary researchers to build, touch and see the workings of artificial minds running on the digital computers of that era.

In the early days of the computational cognitive science domain, emulating the human mind largely followed two primary directions of thought, namely, the top-down approach, and the bottom-up approach.

A few works which followed the former top-down approach are “Logic Theorist” (Newell & Simon, 1956) and “General Problem Solving I” (Newell et al., 1959). These approaches are primarily based on the symbolic approach to artificial intelligence. They are based on the idea that intelligence is a result of symbolic manipulation as in logic and mathematics. The existence of symbols in the human mind is taken as a prior condition for explaining the functioning of the same for different tasks in daily life.

However, contrary to the abovementioned approach, the bottom-up approach drew inspiration from the workings of the nervous system, which works via a “collective interplay of complex ensembles of neurons”.

The first computational model of a biological neuron was introduced by Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts in 1943. This first model could perform boolean operations like AND, OR and NOR logic gates. 

The paper McCulloch and Pitts (1943) states that the nervous system consists of many circular paths, whose activity so regenerates the excitation of any participant neuron that reference to time past becomes indefinite, although it still implies that afferent activity has realized one of a certain class of configurations over time. Precise specification of these implications employing recursive functions, and determination of those that can be embodied in the activity of nervous nets, completes the theory.

In easier words, it means that the excitation of a neuron is the result of cumulative activity over time and can be sustained indefinitely due to the circular nature of the activity propagation paths. Additionally, afferent activity has led to the realization of a certain class of configuration, where Afferent neural activity refers to the neural signals coming into the brain and spinal cord. These logical behaviour of the neurons can be understood in terms of a recursive architecture and represented in the activity of the neurons.

The Theory is called “Nets Without Circles” and there were some physical assumptions related to that as well:

  1. The activity of the neuron is “all-or-none”.
  2. A certain fixed number of synapses must be excited within the period of
    latent addition to excite a neuron at any time.
  3. The number of synapses in the previous assumption is independent of previous activity and position on the neuron.
  4. The only significant delay within the nervous system is synaptic delay.
  5. The activity of any inhibitory synapse prevents excitation of the neuron at that time.
  6. The structure of the net remains unchanged with time.
To present the theory, the authors use symbolism from Language II (Carnap, 1938), augmented with various notations drawn from Principia Mathematica (Russell and Whitehead,1927), including the Principia conventions for dots. Both the above mentioned books are available online in PDF format.

Before proceeding into the discussion of the paper, it should be noted that the authors introduced a — 

functor S, whose value for a property P is the property which holds of a number when P holds of its predecessor; it is defined by “S(P)(t) = P(Kx) . t =x’)”

To elaborate the above statement,

The function S takes the property P as input and returns a property as output. The output property holds for a number x if and only if the property P holds for the predecessor of that number (x).

For example, if P is the property “is even”, then S(P) would be the property “is odd”, because a number n is odd if and only if its predecessor n-1 is even.

However, the mathematical definition “S(P)(t) .=. P(Kx) . t =x’)” given in the paper is not quite understandable to me. From the references used by the author, the above mathematical definition says,

The property given by S(P) at the given argument t is equivalent to the predicate P being applied to the value of the descriptive function K applied on x, with the value of t being equal to x’ indicating Kx is a predecessor to the variable x’.




Some important features of the McCulloch-Pitts Model:
  1. Both the input and output of the neuron are binary.
  2. For a neuron to “fire”, a certain voltage threshold must be passed. The weighted sum of the inputs has to pass this predefined threshold.
  3. The structure and the weights do not change over time.
